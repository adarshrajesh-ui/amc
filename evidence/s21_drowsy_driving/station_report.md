# Station report — shard `s21_drowsy_driving`

**Domain:** motor-vehicle crash mortality attributable to habitual sleep restriction, in a US male aged
16-19. Added during a rework loop because two independent red-team reviewers flagged the injury channel as
a critical omission from a mortality analysis built entirely on chronic-disease cohorts of middle-aged and
elderly adults.

## What I did

1. **Built the dose-response spine.** The task was right that the AAA Foundation analyses are the most
   directly usable numbers in existence: `tefft2016_aaa` is the only source anywhere that reports crash
   rate ratios in exactly the 4-5 h / 5-6 h / 6-7 h bins requested. I hold it in full text and extracted
   all 12 odds ratios, plus its peer-reviewed twin `tefft2018`, plus `connor2002`, plus the only relevant
   meta-analysis, plus `gottlieb2018` and `martiniuk2013`.
2. **Separated acute from habitual exposure, which turned out to be the pivotal analytic decision.** The
   same NMVCCS data yield **OR 1.9 (1.3-2.6)** for having *acutely* slept 5-6 h in the past 24 h and
   **OR 1.4 (0.5-3.6), null**, for *habitually* sleeping 5-6 h. The subject is a habitual short sleeper who
   is not acutely deprived relative to his own baseline on a typical school night, so the habitual figure
   is the like-for-like one. The three habitual estimates in the shard cluster tightly at **1.21, 1.33,
   1.40**, against 1.9-2.7 for acute exposure and at-the-wheel sleepiness. Pricing this subject with acute
   coefficients would have roughly doubled the answer and would have been wrong.
3. **Built the denominator from a census rather than a survey.** IIHS Fatality Facts tabulates FARS by sex
   and single year of age *and separates drivers from passengers*, which matters because only the subject's
   own driving is exposed to his own sleep. Male driver deaths per 100,000 population run 5.3 / 7.5 / 10.5
   at ages 16/17/18 and peak at **13.7 at age 19 — the highest of any age in the human lifespan**. Combined
   with FHWA licensure I get a **1-in-1,800 chance of dying at the wheel during his first three years of
   driving**, cross-checked against NHTSA's independent young-driver count (26.6 per 100,000 licensed male
   drivers 15-20/yr, higher for two identifiable reasons: motorcycles included, older age band).
4. **Discounted for calendar exposure, a step most analyses skip.** His restriction is a school-weekday
   phenomenon, but teen crash deaths concentrate at weekends and in June-August, when he is *not* exposed.
   Only **~41%** of his crash-death risk is incurred while sleep-restricted — not 71% (the weekday share of
   days) and not 100%. Skipping this inflates the answer by a factor of ~2.4.
5. **Resolved the order-of-magnitude discrepancy the task asked about, and reported both ends.** Police
   figures: 1.4% of all crashes, 2.0% of injury crashes, 2.4% of fatal crashes, **633 deaths in 2023**.
   Research figures: 7.0%, 13.1%, 17.6%, **5,967-6,326 deaths/yr**. `tefft2012` resolves it *inside one
   dataset*: drowsiness was unknown for **45% of drivers**, and the same data give 3.6% of fatal crashes
   when unknown is treated as not-drowsy versus **16.5%** imputed. Two unrelated methods agree with each
   other and against the police figure — imputation gives 7.0% of all crashes, and PERCLOS video of
   drivers' eyelids gives 8.8-9.5%. All four reasons for the gap bias the official number downward, so I
   use the research share and report the police share as a floor. **This single choice moves the answer by
   a factor of 7 and is the largest discrete fork in the calculation.**
6. **Computed the answer three independent ways and reported the disagreement.** Route 1 (risk ratio ×
   exposed fraction × causal fraction) gives 0.033 months. Route 2 (drowsy share of fatal crashes ×
   attributable share) gives 0.034. Route 3 (naturalistic drowsy-driving *time* × naturalistic crash odds
   ratio, no self-report and no imputation anywhere) gives **0.011** — three times smaller. I report Route 3
   rather than discarding it, because the reason it is smaller is a genuine constraint on the whole channel:
   a large per-episode risk (OR 6.6) applied to a small share of driving time (1.6% rising to 2.1%) produces
   a small aggregate excess.

## Honesty items, stated plainly

- **The conditioning is load-bearing.** We do not know that this subject drives. ~49% of US males aged
  16-19 are licensed. **If he does not drive, this channel is exactly zero — not small, zero.** I report
  the conditional figure, the non-driver figure, and the licensure-weighted unconditional figure
  (~0.015 months), and I recommend the conditional presentation because it is the informative one.
- **My causal fraction is 0.5 (range 0.25-0.80), and it is a judgement, not a measurement.** Pushing it
  down: CDC's 50,370-student YRBS analysis shows short-sleeping US high-school students drink and drive,
  ride with drinking drivers, skip seatbelts and text while driving; the **seatbelt** pathway inflates the
  *fatal*-crash association specifically without any vigilance mechanism; the same analysis **fails a
  negative control**, because students sleeping ≥10 h show the same risk behaviours; `cummings2001` reports
  that **playing a radio halved crash risk**, which is residual confounding in the same models that produced
  the sleep estimates; and smoking predicts young-driver crashes as strongly as drowsiness on two
  continents. Holding it up: `gottlieb2018` adjusts for **miles driven** — the dominant confounder — and
  the association survives *and strengthens* among people who do not report sleepiness; `martiniuk2013` is
  prospective with a **police-reported** outcome and adjusts for risky driving, substance use and hours
  driven; and the mechanism is directly **observed on video**, not inferred.
- **I did not oversell the school-start-time studies, and they came out against me.** Five analyses agree
  in sign and disagree by a factor of six in magnitude. **The two designs with genuine pre-post
  identification give the two smallest estimates (RR ≈ 1.07 and 1.16, the latter *not significant* at
  p = .076); the two pure two-place cross-sections give the two largest (1.27-1.41).** That ordering is
  what between-place confounding looks like. Worse for the drowsiness hypothesis, the only study with real
  identification ran the decisive mechanism test and it failed: crashes fell 25% in the 07:00 hour and rose
  21% in the 08:00 hour, fell 48% at 14:00 and rose 32% at 15:00 — **crashes moved with the commute rather
  than disappearing** — with *"no meaningful change in early morning or nighttime crashes, when
  drowsiness-induced crashes might have been expected to be most common."* And `vorona2014`'s own mechanism
  test came out **backwards**, with significantly *more* run-off-road-right crashes in the *late*-start
  county. I therefore treat this literature as establishing **sign only**. It is the main reason my causal
  fraction is 0.5 rather than 0.9. In fairness the other way: these studies test a 20-45 minute dose shift,
  not the 1.5-2.5 h separating this subject from a rested sleeper, so a small noisy effect from a small dose
  is not evidence against a larger effect from a larger dose.
- **The uncertainty spans more than an order of magnitude and I am reporting a range, not a false point
  estimate.** 14× across the plausible range, 58× across the extremes. Ranked by contribution: drowsy share
  of fatal crashes (7×), causal fraction (3.2×), risk ratio (2.1×), baseline and licensure (1.8×).
- **My own headline argues against the reviewers who commissioned this shard, and I have not softened it.**
  They suspected this channel might dominate the subject's mortality. It does not. Two things in the
  evidence explain why, and I extracted both rather than burying them: the sleep-medicine field's own
  consensus panel puts probable impairment at **3-5 h** and unfitness to drive at **≤2 h**, both *below*
  this subject's routine 5.5-6 h; and the like-for-like habitual coefficients are 1.2-1.4, not 2.5.
- **De-duplication warnings are set, and one of them matters more than the estimate itself.** Within the
  shard, `tefft2016_aaa`/`tefft2018` are the same NMVCCS drivers; `vorona2011`/`vorona2014`/`binhasan2020`
  all use Virginia DMV data with overlapping jurisdictions (`binhasan2020`'s "rest of Virginia" control
  *contains* the other two studies' treated units); `owens2018_aaa` and `ghsa2026`'s 1.57% are both SHRP2;
  `cummings2001` and `connor2002` are both inside `bioulac2017`'s pool. **Across shards:** `s13_mortality`
  applied a hazard ratio to *all-cause* adolescent mortality, which already contains motor-vehicle deaths.
  My per-capita driver-death probability is 11.7% of all-cause adolescent male mortality, so part of this
  channel is already inside their number and **must not be added twice**. Details in `injury_channel.md` §9.

## The comparison to 0.2 months, and a correction to the framing

**Smaller, by roughly 6-7×.** 0.03 months against 0.2. No parameterisation I regard as defensible reaches
0.2; only simultaneously pushing the baseline, the exposed fraction, the risk ratio and the causal fraction
to their individual extremes gets to 0.21.

But **0.2 months is not the project's chronic-disease estimate — it is `s13_mortality`'s explicitly labelled
un-discounted ceiling.** That shard's own central exposure-window figure is **0.006-0.05 months**. Against
the number the project actually estimated, this channel is **comparable in size and probably larger**, and
after the de-duplication above it roughly **doubles** the credible exposure-window mortality total. So the
reviewers were right that the channel was missing and wrong about its size.

Two further points the downstream report should carry:

- **This term is much better-founded than the chronic-disease one.** The chronic-disease estimate rests on
  transporting hazard ratios from 60-year-olds across a 40-year gap through a residue fraction that `s13`
  itself grades D and says must carry prior mass at zero. This term rests on a FARS census denominator,
  three independent estimation routes that agree within a factor of three, a mechanism observed directly on
  video, and — uniquely among the project's mortality channels — **an effect that acts during the exposure
  window, so no persistence assumption is needed at all**. The deaths either happened between 16 and 19 or
  they did not. It is a small number that we actually know.
- **Neither mortality channel is where the damage from this exposure lives.** Both are hundredths to tenths
  of a month. If the project's headline is driven by mortality, the headline is being driven by its smallest
  and least certain component.

## Non-fatal serious outcomes

Virginia DMV administrative data across three records put police-reported crash involvement for licensed
16-18-year-olds at **29.6-71.2 per 1,000 per year** (central ~45/1,000 = 4.5%/yr, ~13.5% over three years).
Applying the all-crash drowsy share and the causal fraction gives an absolute **~1-in-80 chance of a
drowsy-involved police-reported crash over the three years, of which about half (~1 in 160) is attributable
to the sleep restriction** — roughly **130× more likely than the excess death**. `tefft2012` shows drowsy
crashes are more severe than average (13.1% of hospital-admission crashes vs 7.0% of all crashes involve a
drowsy driver), so the excess serious-injury count is enriched relative to the excess crash count. **I could
not put a number on excess hospitalisations** because I do not hold a teen hospitalisations-per-crash base
rate, and I did not invent one.

## What I could not find or could not get

1. **There is no meta-analysis of habitual sleep DURATION and crash risk.** Four query formulations
   returned 0, 0, 2 and 2 PubMed hits, all obstructive sleep apnoea or hypnotic-drug reviews. The one
   relevant meta-analysis, `bioulac2017`, pools **sleepiness at the wheel** (OR 2.51), which is a different
   and much more proximal exposure. The task asked me to look; the honest answer is that the pooled
   dose-response the downstream model most wants has never been produced.
2. **Dingus 2016 (PNAS, SHRP2) is the one retrieval I wanted and could not get.** Not open access; Europe
   PMC returned an empty body and NCBI efetch returned front matter only. Its drowsiness odds ratio is in
   the body, not the abstract, and I will not cite a number I did not read. It is the same SHRP2 cohort as
   `owens2018_aaa`, so it would have been cohort-duplicated rather than independent.
3. **CDC WONDER rejected my query.** The API returned `Invalid 'Year/Month' codes` across several
   formulations. Rather than keep guessing at an undocumented code vocabulary I substituted IIHS/FARS,
   which is strictly better for this purpose because it is already stratified by sex, single year of age
   **and driver-versus-passenger**. No number in this shard depends on WONDER.
4. **Eighteen of 24 records are abstract-only**, because the crash-safety literature sits behind Elsevier,
   Oxford, JAMA and BMJ paywalls and most of it has no PMC deposit. Every extracted number in those records
   appears verbatim in the quoted abstract; I took nothing from paper bodies I did not hold. The costs are
   specific and recorded: I could not retrieve `connor2002`'s covariate list or age-stratified analyses,
   `martiniuk2013`'s covariate list, or `foss2019`'s ARIMA coefficients. **The sharpest single loss** is
   `cummings2001`'s sleep-hours effect: its ≤9 h-per-48 h threshold against a 12 h referent is almost
   exactly this subject's contrast, and the abstract states the direction but **publishes no point estimate
   or interval**, so under hard rule 2 I could not extract it.
5. **No study anywhere gives a fatal-crash risk ratio for habitual short sleep in adolescents.** Every
   usable relative risk in this shard has an **all-crash** or **injury-crash** outcome. Converting to a
   fatal-crash risk ratio requires assuming the sleep effect is constant across severities. That assumption
   is probably conservative — `tefft2018` shows the effect concentrates in single-vehicle crashes, which
   are disproportionately fatal, and 41% of fatal crashes involving 16-19-year-old drivers are
   single-vehicle — but it is an assumption, and I have flagged it rather than importing it silently.

## The single biggest gap in the evidence for this domain

**No study has ever measured crash risk as a function of habitual sleep duration in adolescents with an
externally-verified crash outcome and adequate control for driving exposure.** Every component of that
sentence is missing from something in the literature:

- The best **habitual** estimate in young drivers (`martiniuk2013`, RR 1.21) does not report whether it
  adjusted for miles driven, and dichotomises exposure at 6 h so there is no dose-response.
- The best **exposure-adjusted** estimate (`gottlieb2018`, OR 1.13/hour) is in adults with a mean age near
  60, uses **self-reported** crashes, and publishes no CI for the 6 h-versus-7-8 h contrast.
- The best **dose-resolved** estimates (`tefft2016_aaa`) come from a dataset that **excluded every crash
  between midnight and 05:59** — the hours where drowsy crashes concentrate — and its habitual-sleep cell
  at 5-6 h contains only 102 drivers, which is why that CI runs 0.5-3.6.
- The best **objective** exposure measurement (`owens2018_aaa`, PERCLOS video) contains **no fatal crashes
  at all**, by the authors' own statement.
- The only **quasi-experimental** leverage tests a 20-45 minute dose shift and cannot separate reduced
  drowsiness from reduced night-time driving exposure — the authors say so explicitly.

Consequently the causal fraction, which moves the answer by 3.2×, rests on judgement rather than
identification. There is **no Mendelian randomisation** for sleep duration with a crash outcome and **no
randomised trial** of adolescent sleep duration with a crash outcome. One trial that would help was found
and is registered — a pragmatic RCT of improving young-driver sleep (PMID 38463828) — but at screening it
had published only a protocol. **A single RCT reporting crash outcomes, or an age-stratified drowsy share
of fatal crashes from the AAA CISS→FARS model, would do more for this estimate than any number of further
observational studies.**
