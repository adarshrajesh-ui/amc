 Youth suicide has been increasing and became a public health concern worldwide. Identifying insufficient sleep as the potential risk factor is critical to reducing suicide risk and increasing trends. This study aimed to determine whether insufficient sleep is associated with increasing trends in suicidal behaviors and disparities by sex, age, and race/ethnicity among school adolescents.
 Methods:
 The current study used biennial data from the US nationally representative Youth Risk Behavior Survey(YRBS) from 2007 to 2019. Joinpoint regression models were used to estimate biennial percent changes (BPCs) and average BPCs (ABPCs) of suicidal behaviors by sleep duration. Logistic regression models were used to examine the association between insufficient sleep and suicidal behaviors.
 Results:
 Of 73356 adolescent students included(mean[SD]age,16.11[1.23]years), 50.03% were female. Suicidal ideation and suicide plan among the insufficient sleep group increased from 2007 to 2019 (BPC = 2.54% [95%CI: 1.30%, 3.79%]; BPC = 3.20%[95% CI: 1.77%, 4.64%]), but were non-significant among the sufficient sleep group. Trends in suicidal ideation(ABPC=3.03%[95%CI:1.35%,4.73%]) and suicide plan(ABPC=4.03%[95%CI:2.47%,5.62%]) among female adolescents with insufficient sleep increased, but non-significant among male adolescents with insufficient sleep. Suicidal ideation(ABPC=1.73%[95%CI:0.51%,2.97%]) and suicide plan(ABPC=2.31%[95%CI:0.70%,3.95%]) increased among younger adolescents only with insufficient sleep; whereas suicide trends by sleep duration were similar among older adolescents. Suicide plan among insufficient sleep group increased across the four racial groups, with BPC highest for the White (BPC = 3.48% [95% CI: 1.31%, 5.69%]), and lowest for the Hispanic/Latino (BPC = 1.18% [95% CI: 0.15%, 2.23%]), but were nonsignificant among sufficient sleep group except for the White (BPC = 2.83% [95% CI:0.62%, 5.09%]).
 Conclusions:
 Insufficient sleep was disproportionately associated with increasing trends in suicidal behaviors among female, younger, and non-White adolescent students. Ensuring sufficient sleep can potentially reduce suicide among school adolescents.

 Suicide in adolescents is a public health concern worldwide. In the US, suicide is the second leading cause of death among adolescents aged 10–14 years and 15–24 years( 1 , 2 ). Visits to hospital emergency departments for suicidal ideation or suicide attempts doubled among US youth aged 5 to 18 years between 2007 and 2015( 3 ). Although the US has greatly invested in youth suicide prevention( 4 ), compelling evidence shows clear increases in suicidal behaviors in the past decade, with female, younger, and non-white adolescents disproportionately affected( 5 ). Therefore, urgent efforts are needed to find modifiable risk factors, in particular of insufficient sleep for adolescent suicide and address this alarming public health issue.
 Sufficient sleep has indispensable benefits for adolescent mental health, whereas over 70% of US high school students do not get the recommended sleep of at least 8 hours on school nights( 6 ). Emerging studies suggest worldwide adolescents report sleeping less and less( 6 ); particularly, 30% fewer adolescents in the US reported ≥7 hours of sleep in 2012 compared to 1991( 7 ). Insufficient sleep has been consistently linked to depressive mood, unsafe behaviors, and suicide among US youth( 8 ). Substantial evidence has suggested insufficient sleep as a proximal and modifiable risk factor for suicidal behaviors, independent of depression and suicidal history( 9 ). Indeed, one hour less sleep has been linked to an increased risk of hopelessness, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and substance use among adolescents( 10 ). Furthermore, previous studies showed sex, age, and race/ethnicity differences in suicide, sleep duration, and their associations among adolescents( 11 ). While the risk of insufficient sleep linked to adolescent suicidal behaviors needs to be replicated in the context of temporal change, whether insufficient sleep is associated with increasing trends in suicidal behaviors and disparities by sex, age, and race/ethnicity among adolescents remains uncertain.
 Therefore, adding to previous studies that predominantly explored the individual-level relationship between insufficient sleep and suicidal behaviors( 12 ), this study utilized data from the US nationally representative Youth Risk Behavior Survey from 2007 to 2019 to investigate divergent trends in suicidal behaviors and disparities by sex, age, and race/ethnicity among school adolescents over the recent decade by sleep duration. We hypothesized that insufficient sleep would be associated with increasing trends in suicidal behaviors and disparities by sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Such endeavor will inform innovative and effective suicide prevention and intervention strategies tailored to adolescents at different risks.
 METHODS
 Data and Participants
 The Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) is an independent nationally representative survey of high school students in grades 9 through 12 attending public and private schools in the US. It is a series of independently selected cross-sectional datasets and conducted biennially between 1991 and 2019, with parental permission obtained for each locality. Each independent protocol of YRBS was approved by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Institutional Review Board, and the database is publicly available ( 13 ).
 We obtained pooled YRBS data from 2007 to 2019 that based on the same survey methodology. Unweighted sample sizes ranged from 13,583 to 16,410; school response rates ranged from 75% to 81%; student response rates ranged from 80% to 88%, and overall survey response rates ranged from 60% to 71%. We included 73,356 participants who reported on their sleep duration and four suicidal behaviors in the past year, due to 30,169 missing information on suicidal behaviors, sleep duration, sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Details of sampling strategies and reliability of the YRBS survey items are reported elsewhere( 14 ).
 The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline for cross-sectional studies.
 Measures
 Suicidal behaviors
 Four self-reported suicidal behaviors are assessed on the YRBS: (1) suicidal ideation: “During the past 12 months, did you ever seriously consider attempting suicide?”; (2) suicide plan: “During the past 12 months, did you make a plan about how you would attempt suicide?”; (3) suicide attempts: “During the past 12 months, how many times did you attempt suicide?”; and (4) injury by suicide attempts: “If you attempted suicide during the past 12 months, did any attempt result in an injury, poisoning, or overdose that had to be treated by a doctor or nurse?” Response options were dichotomized into yes/no for analysis.
 Sleep duration
 Youth were asked about sleep duration with the question, “On an average school night, how many hours of sleep do you get?” with responses of ≤4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, and ≥10 hours. Responses were dichotomized into insufficient sleep (<8h) and sufficient sleep (≥8h) based on recommendations of optimal sleep hours for adolescents( 15 ).
 Demographics and covariates
 Demographics included sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Sex was dichotomized as male and female. The age groups (≤12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, ≥18 years) were dichotomized into younger (≤15 years) and older groups (>15 years)( 16 ). Four categories of race/ethnicity in YRBS were White, Black, Hispanic/Latino, and all other races.
 According to pertinent literature( 17 ), covariates included depressive symptom, being overweight, cigarette smoking, alcohol use, marijuana use, and illicit drug use in 2007 to 2019 surveys. Additional covariates, namely cyberbullying victimization, physical activity, and traditional bullying victimization were also included when available in 2011 to 2019 surveys. These covariates were all dichotomized for the analyses( eFigure 2 , 3 )
 Statistical Analysis
 We conducted 3 main analyses. First, we calculated the weighted prevalence of insufficient sleep and four suicidal behaviors, separated by sleep duration and sex, age, race/ethnicity using Stata/SE 15 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX USA). Second, Joinpoint version 4.9.0.0 (National Cancer Institute) was used to estimate piecewise log-linear trends in the survey-weighted prevalence estimates of insufficient sleep and suicidal behaviors over time( 18 ). Trends are presented as linear segments connected at the years (i.e., Joinpoint) when the slope of the trend changes significantly. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided P value <0.05 with Wald χ 2 tests using design-adjusted coefficient variance-covariance matrices for the joinpoint regression analyses. Joinpoint can provide biennial percent change (BPC, used for straight lines) and average BPC (ABPC, the average biennial percent change for the identified linear segment with Joinpoint). If there is no Joinpoint, the ABPC is equal to the BPC for that segment. Trends in four suicidal behaviors were stratified by sleep duration and sex, age, and race/ethnicity. Third, we used logistic regression to estimated adjusted odds ratios (aOR) for suicidal behaviors linked to insufficient sleep, adjusting for corresponding covariates for the pooled sample and each year sample.
 Intersectional terms of sleep duration, sex, age, and race/ethnicity were constructed (i.e., sleep duration × sex, sleep duration × age, and sleep duration × race/ethnicity) to account for within-group differences in suicidal behaviors. The Bonferroni correction was consider for the logistic regression analyses in the pooled sample, and statistical significance was determined by a two-sided p =0.003 (i.e., 0.05/16) as an approximate adjustment for 16 comparisons (4 logistics regression models of 4 outcomes).
 We adjusted for complex sampling and survey weights to obtain US nationally representative estimates, accounting for non-response and oversampling of Black and Hispanic/Latino students. Data analysis was conducted from February to May 2023.
 RESULTS
 Sample Characteristics
 Sample characteristics in each year are presented in Table 1 . Among the 73,356 adolescents, 37,574 were female(weighted percentage, 50.03%[95%CI: 49.04%, 51.01%]) and 24,820 were ≤15 years(weighted percentage, 36.05%[95%CI: 35.32%, 36.78%]). Regarding racial/ethnic groups, 34,641 were White(weighted percentage, 60.13%[95%CI: 57.62%, 62.58%]), 10,927 were Black(weighted percentage, 11.53%[95%CI: 10.44%, 12.73%]), 20,190 were Hispanic/Latino(weighted percentage, 19.55%[95%CI: 17.84%, 21.39%]), and 7,598 were other races(weighted percentage, 8.79%[95%CI: 7.96%, 9.69%]).
 Trends in Suicidal Behaviors by Sleep Duration
 Weighted prevalence increased from 69.24%(95%CI: 67.14%, 71.27%) in 2007 to 78.69%(95%CI: 76.96%, 80.32%) in 2019 for insufficient sleep ( Table 1 ). Joinpoint analysis showed a significant increase from 2013 to 2019 (BPC=2.30%[95%CI: 1.03%, 3.58%], p =0.016), resulting in an overall ABPC of 1.06 %(95%CI: 0.66%, 1.46%, p< 0.001) from 2007 to 2019( eTable 2 ).
 Weighted prevalence rates of suicidal ideation, suicide plan, suicide attempts, and injury by suicide attempts were higher in 2019 than in 2007 for both adolescents with sufficient sleep and insufficient sleep( Table 1 ). However, Joinpoint regression showed suicidal ideation and suicide plan in adolescents with insufficient sleep increased from 2007 to 2019 (BPC=2.54%[95%CI:1.30%, 3.79%], p =0.003; BPC=3.20% [95%CI: 1.77%, 4.64%], p =0.002), but were non-significantly increasing with sufficient sleep ( eTable 2 ). Moreover, Joinpoint regression showed that suicide attempts and injury by suicide attempts in adolescents with insufficient and sufficient sleep were non-significantly increasing from 2007 to 2019( eTable 2 ).
 Multivariate logistic regression showed that adolescents with insufficient sleep were more likely to report suicidal ideation (aOR=1.38[95%CI: 1.28, 1.48], p <0.001), suicide plan (aOR=1.34[95%CI: 1.23, 1.46], p <0.001), and suicide attempts(aOR=1.24[95%CI: 1.17, 1.38], p <0.001) than those with sufficient sleep ( Figure 1 ). Moreover, the risk of suicidal behaviors were significantly higher among adolescents with insufficient sleep than those with sufficient sleep for each wave survey( p <0.05, excluding injury by suicide attempts( eFigure 1 ).
 Trends in Suicidal Behaviors by Sleep Duration and Sex
 Joinpoint regression showed that trends in suicidal ideation(ABPC=3.03%[95%CI: 1.35%, 4.73%], p =0.005) and suicide plan(ABPC=4.03%[95%CI:2.47%, 5.62%], p =0.001) among female adolescents with insufficient sleep increased from 2007 to 2019, but non-significantly increasing among male adolescents with insufficient sleep ( eTable 2 ). As for suicide attempts and injury by suicide attempts, the trends in adolescents with sufficient and insufficient sleep were non-significantly change in both females and males from 2007 to 2019( Figure 2 , eTable 3 ).
 However, we found that female adolescents with insufficient sleep were at greatest risk of suicidal ideation (OR=1.46[95%CI:1.27, 1.69], p <0.001) and suicide plan (OR=1.44[95%CI:1.25, 1.66], p <0.001) among subgroup of sleep duration and sex interaction between 2007 and 2019 ( Figure 1 ).
 Trends in Suicidal behaviors by Sleep Duration and Age
 Joinpoint regression showed that in younger adolescents, suicidal ideation (ABPC=1.73%[95%CI:0.51%, 2.97%], p =0.015), and suicide plan (ABPC=2.31%[95%CI:0.70%, 3.95%], p =0.014) increased among those with insufficient sleep, but not among those with sufficient sleep ( eTable 3 ). In older adolescents, trends in suicidal ideation(ABPC=2.82% [95%CI:1.56%, 4.09%], p =0.002; ABPC=2.47% [95%CI:0.31%, 4.67%], p =0.032) and suicide plan(ABPC=3.56% [95%CI:1.89%, 5.27%], p =0.003; ABPC=2.21% [95%CI:0.01%, 4.46%], p =0.049) among those with insufficient sleep and sufficient sleep all significantly increased( eTable 3 ). Trends in suicide attempts and injury by suicide attempts were non-significantly increasing among older adolescents with insufficient or sufficient sleep. Trend in suicide attempts only increased among younger adolescents with insufficient sleep(ABPC=0.91%[95%CI:0.01%, 1.82%], p =0.049), and there was non-significantly increasing among older counterparts( Figure 3 , eTable 3 ).
 In addition, we found that younger adolescents with insufficient sleep were 1,33-fold (95%CI: 1.22, 1.46, p <0.001) more likely to report suicidal ideation than older adolescents with sufficient sleep over time ( Figure 1 ).
 Trends in Suicidal Behaviors by Sleep Duration and Race/ethnicity
 Joinpoint regression showed that in White adolescents, suicidal ideation and suicide plan with insufficient sleep(ABPC=3.13%[95%CI:1.75%, 4.52%], p =0.002; ABPC=3.48%[95%CI:1.31%, 5.69%], p =0.009) increased more than those with sufficient sleep(BPC=2.40% [95%CI:0.24%, 4.61%], p =0.035; BPC=2.83% [95%CI:0.62%, 5.09%], p =0.021)( Figure 4 , eTable 3 ). In other races, suicidal ideation with insufficient sleep increased(BPC=1.96%[95%CI:0.43%, 3.52%], p =0.021), but was non-significantly falling with sufficient sleep. Suicide plan among adolescents with insufficient sleep increased across the four racial groups, with BPC highest for the White (BPC = 3.48% [95% CI: 1.31%, 5.69%], p = .009), and lowest for the Hispanic/Latino (BPC = 1.18% [95% CI: 0.15%, 2.23%], p = .032), but were nonsignificantly changed among adolescents with sufficient sleep except for the White (BPC = 2.83% [95% CI: 0.62%, 5.09%], p = .021) ( Figure 4 , Table S3 ).
 Trends in suicide attempts among adolescents with insufficient sleep only increased among White adolescents from 2007 to 2019 (BPC=2.38%[95%CI:0.32%, 4.49%], p =0.031). Trends of injury by suicide attempts among adolescents with insufficient sleep increased only in Black youth(BPC=4.70%[95%CI:2.81%, 6.62%], p =0.001)( Figure 4 , eTable 3 ).
 However, all other races group adolescents with insufficient sleep were 1.83-fold (95%CI: 1.58, 2.12, p <0.001), 1.73-fold (95%CI: 1.48, 2.03, p <0.001) and 2.22-fold(95%CI: 1.83, 2.69, p <0.001) more likely to report suicidal ideation, suicide plan and suicide attempts than white adolescents with sufficient sleep over time( Figure 1 ).
 DISCUSSION
 This study delineates the distinct relationship between insufficient sleep and the latest trends in suicidal behaviors by sex, age, and race/ethnicity among US school adolescents from 2007 to 2019( 1 ). We found that insufficient sleep has increased by 10% around from 69.25% to 78.67% over the 12-year period, with a significant and accelerated increase by 2.30% biennially after 2013. More importantly, we revealed the increasing trends of suicidal ideation and suicide plan among adolescents with insufficient sleep, but were non-significantly increasing among those with sufficient sleep. We further found the increasing trends of suicidal ideation and suicide plan only among female, younger and non-White adolescents with insufficient sleep. There is also a significant increase of suicide attempts among younger adolescents with insufficient sleep. These increasing trends of suicidal ideation, suicide plan, and suicide attempts were not evident in their counterparts with sufficient sleep. Furthermore, insufficient sleep was consistently associated with an increased risk of suicidal ideation, suicide plan, suicide attempts, and injury by suicide attempts from 2007 to 2019, adjusting for a wide range of covariates. These findings offer novel insights into the association between insufficient sleep and the rising trends of suicidal behaviors among school adolescents, and thus inform prevention and intervention strategies prioritizing sleep health, especially among female, younger and non-White adolescents.
 We found insufficient sleep among US adolescents has increased significantly since 2013, a turning point that is consistent with previous findings showing drastically increase in social media usage among adolescents since 2012( 19 ). Social media usage can directly disrupt circadian rhythms through blue light produced by electronic devices, and lead to reduced sleep duration( 20 ). More importantly, this study found that suicidal ideation and suicide plan among adolescents with insufficient sleep increased from 2007 to 2019, but remained stable among those with sufficient sleep. Given previous suicide prevention strategies have not sufficiently thwarted the suicide increase in US youth over decades( 21 ), this finding is significant to indicate an innovative approach that integrates promoting sufficient sleep may reduce increasing trends in suicidal ideation and suicide plan.
 Sleep deprivation, especially when accompanied by circadian misalignment (i.e., a mismatch between circadian-mediated sleep patterns and environmental demands such as early school start times), may compromise prefrontal cortex function and related executive functions and emotional regulation that are in their most rapid evolution during adolescence, and thus increase risk for suicidal ideation and suicide plan( 22 ). It’s crucial to note that individuals who have considered or planned suicide may already be experiencing negative life events, mental disorders, and physical health issues( 23 – 25 ), and thus often have insufficient sleep( 26 , 27 ). From a clinical perspective, it is of great value to assess and intervene insufficient sleep and suicidal behaviors in school adolescents together, considering their bidirectional effects.
 It is interesting that we found either trends in suicide attempts or injury by suicide attempts among adolescents were non-significantly increasing, despite sleep duration. Epidemiological evidence on the relationship of insufficient sleep with suicidal ideation, suicide plan, suicide attempts and injury by suicide attempts remains controversial. Some studies have shown that insufficient sleep is associated with increased risk for both suicidal ideation and suicide attempts( 28 ). However, a cross-sectional study from a Korean representative sample found that insufficient sleep increased the likelihood of suicidal ideation but not of suicide attempts( 29 ). Conversely, one longitudinal study from the Netherlands confirmed that short sleep was associated with increased suicide attempts rather than suicidal ideation( 30 ). Nevertheless, evidence on how insufficient sleep is associated with trends of different suicidal behaviors is still lacking, which requires further investigations. Considering suicidal ideation and suicide plan are often early signs of suicide attempts and injury by suicide attempts( 31 ) and their increasing trend linked to insufficient sleep, ensuring adolescent sleep needs might have potential benefit in reducing suicide attempts and injuries among adolescents.
 We further identified sex, age, and race/ethnicity differences with regard to trends in suicidal behaviors among adolescents associated with insufficient sleep, potentially adding to our knowledge to identify vulnerable populations to prioritize and tailor prevention and invention strategies. Consistent with previous findings( 32 ), we found females demonstrated increasing trends of suicidal ideation and suicide plan, whereas males demonstrated an increasing trend of suicide attempts. Importantly, our study further showed that female adolescents with insufficient sleep, but not sufficient sleep, reported increasing trends of suicidal ideation and suicide plan, while no such difference in sleep duration was evident among male adolescents. This is consistent with the early finding in Korean adolescents indicating that insufficient sleep disproportionately predisposes female adolescents to an increased risk of suicidal ideation and suicide plan. Sex disparities in sleep duration may lie in differences in the metabolism of sex hormones and sleep-associated neurotransmitters( 33 ). Furthermore, insufficient sleep is more prevalent in female adolescents in this study, and thus they may be at higher risk of suicidal ideation and suicide plan through physiological and psychological mechanisms( 34 ). In addition, time spent on social media, a well-recognized correlate of insufficient sleep has been increasing and linked to higher suicide risk in female teens but not males( 35 ). While there has been an increase in suicidal behaviors in adolescents aged 12–15 years compared to those aged 16–18 years in the US( 21 ), the current study revealed a greater increase in suicidal ideation among older adolescents aged >15 years. Our further analyses identified significant increases in suicidal ideation, suicide plan, and suicide attempts among younger adolescents with insufficient sleep, but not with sufficient sleep, whereas no sleep disparities were observed among older adolescents. Notably, only younger adolescents with insufficient sleep showed significant increases in suicide attempts.
 Race/ethnicity disparities in suicide trends have been noted in US adolescents, with Black adolescents showing the highest rates of suicide attempts and injury by suicide attempts [ 16 ]. Our further analyses identified significant increases in suicide plan among Black, Hispanic/Latino, and all other races adolescents with insufficient sleep, but not with sufficient sleep, whereas no sleep disparities were observed among White adolescents. Moreover, Black adolescents with insufficient sleep were the only group demonstrating significant increases in injury by suicide attempts in the current analysis. Taken together, race/ethnicity was more likely to influence trends in suicidal behaviors among adolescents in the context of insufficient sleep. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that racial and ethnic minority adolescents, are at a higher risk of insufficient sleep( 36 ) and are more vulnerable to sleep-related functional impairments( 37 ). It is possible that non-White adolescents are disproportionately living in disadvantaged neighborhoods with less safety and connectedness, which increased their psychological distress, reduced their sleep hours, and increased suicide risks( 38 ). The association between race/ethnicity and insufficient sleep was an important social determinant increasing the trends of suicidal behaviors and the underlying mechanism warrants further investigations.
 Insufficient sleep among adolescents is a potentially modifiable target for suicide prevention. Our findings highlight the importance of including sleep in suicide surveillance, prevention, and intervention, especially in more vulnerable populations. Adolescents and all stakeholders including parents, school officials, healthcare professionals, and policymakers can play an important role in establishing and monitoring healthy sleep habits. In order to improve health, academic performance, and quality of life, delaying middle and high school start times to 8:30 am or later would allow adolescents the opportunity to obtain sufficient sleep and the timing of sleep in accordance with their circadian rhythms and reduce risks of suicide( 39 ).
 It is crucial to note, however, that reporting changes of trend in suicidal behaviors and insufficient sleep could be driven by multiple factors, including expansions in health insurance coverage for mental illness( 40 ) and screening( 41 ), changes in the way that mental health problems are diagnosed and coded in medical records( 42 ), declining social stigma associated with mental illness( 43 ), increased willingness to discuss suicidal behaviors, and a concurrent increased willingness to seek care( 44 ). Our study did not intend to test these mechanisms, which are important future steps.
 The limitation of the study is the cross-sectional nature of YRBS data which precludes causal inference from reported associations. It’s also unable to track individuals who may have died by suicide attempts, introducing the potential for selection bias. Additionally, the reliance on self-reported data could have introduced bias due to social desirability or recall bias, and future studies may consider adding a more objective measure of sleep duration, such as actigraphy. The YRBS does not include a measure of circadian preference (chronotype), which would have been very useful in assessing sleep-related risk in the future. It also does not include variables related to nonsuicidal self-injury, but sleep disturbances are relevant to the broader context of self-injurious behaviors( 45 ). There may have been other confounders, including poor socioeconomic conditions, anxiety, exposure to child abuse/adverse childhood experiences, which could have accounted for the increase in suicide and insufficient sleep( 46 , 47 ). Moreover, other sleep disturbances (i.e., trouble falling asleep, early morning awakening, hypersomnia, and nightmares) related to suicide( 48 , 49 ) should be considered when interpreting our findings. Lastly, the findings from US high school adolescents may not be generalizable to all adolescents, such as those who are not attending schools or in other cultures.
 CONCLUSIONS
 This study provided new evidence that insufficient sleep was associated with increasing trends in suicidal behaviors by sex, age, and race/ethnicity among US high school adolescents from 2007 to 2019. The findings highlight prevention and intervention strategies prioritizing sleep health to reduce adolescent suicidal behaviors and socially determined disparities. Sleep deprivation and increasing suicidal rates are global public health challenges for adolescents in other countries. Further research will be necessary to generalize our findings to other countries and regions, and develop nation-specific and culture-tailored strategies, which should involve multi-sectoral efforts, and reach vulnerable populations.
