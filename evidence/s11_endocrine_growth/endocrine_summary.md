# Endocrine and growth consequences — s11_endocrine_growth

Subject: male, 18 y at assessment, weekday sleep ~5–6 h from age 16 to 19, weekend/holiday 7–8 h,
occasional 3–4 h pre-exam nights, occasional 10–11 h weekend nights.

Every verdict below carries a number and an interval. Where an interval is *not* a published
confidence interval — because I interpolated between doses or derived it from a measurement floor —
that is stated explicitly in the line itself.

---

## Verdict 1 — Growth hormone: REDISTRIBUTION, not loss

**Change in 24-h GH output at 5–6 h/night: 0%. Interval −32% to +32%, and that interval is a
*detection floor*, not an observed effect: no study has ever measured a reduction in 24-h GH output
under sleep restriction.**

The sleep-dependent share of daily GH output in men is large and real: 52.8% ± 3.5% of 24-h GH
production comes from the single sleep-onset pulse (95% CI 45.9–59.7%, n=10 day-active men, 10-min
sampling over 24 h — Brandenberger 2004), and a narrative synthesis of the same literature puts it at
"approximately 70%" (Van Cauter 1998). Roughly 69% of GH pulses during sleep are coupled to slow-wave
stages (57 of 83 pulses, 95% CI 58–78%, n=8 — Van Cauter 1992). So the premise of the worry is correct.

The inference does not follow, for three reasons that each have a number attached.

**(a) When the sleep pulse is blunted, waking pulses replace it, and the 24-h total does not move.**
In 11 night workers sleeping 07:00–15:00, "the sleep-related GH pulse was lowered, but the reduction
was compensated for by the emergence of large individual pulses occurring unpredictably during waking
periods, so that the total amount of GH secreted during the 24 h was constant" (Brandenberger 2004).
The authors extend this explicitly to sleep deprivation. Under 6 nights of 4 h in bed the GH profile
became *biphasic*, with a new pre-sleep pulse that was **negatively correlated** with the post-sleep
pulse — the signature of a trade-off, not a loss (n=11 — Spiegel 2000). A review that read that
paper's full text reports the direction of the 24-h change: "The elevation in GH levels observed by
Spiegel et al. was the result of an extended period of nocturnal GH secretion" (Magee 2010). GH went
**up**, via a lengthened secretory window.

**(b) Slow-wave sleep — the GH-permissive stage — is the last thing sacrificed.** Cutting time in bed
from 10 h to 5 h for 8 nights cost 165 min of stage-2 sleep per night (95% CI 144–186) and 63 min of
REM (95% CI 50–76), but **gained 9 min of stage 3+4** (95% CI 3.3–14.7 min, P=.01, n=10 — Leproult
2011). Spiegel 2000 found the same: "When sleep was restricted, both SWS and SWA were increased during
early sleep." A 5-hour night contains essentially all of a normal night's slow-wave sleep. The
substrate for the sleep-onset GH pulse is intact.

**(c) The signal is smaller than the noise.** Total 24-h GH output varies 10-fold between men, and the
within-subject coefficient of variation across repeated 24-h profiles is 32% (Van Cauter 1992, n=8 ×
6 sessions each). At n=8–11 no study in this literature could detect a true 24-h GH reduction below
roughly 30%.

For scale: normal ageing from the 16–25 y band to midlife removes 372 µg of 24-h GH secretion per
decade (P<.001, n=149 — Van Cauter 2000), as slow-wave sleep falls from 18.9% (SEM 1.3%) of the sleep
period at 16–25 y to 3.4% (SEM 1.0%) at 36–50 y. The subject sits at the lifetime *peak* of
slow-wave sleep. Van Cauter 2000 is frequently cited as evidence that sleep loss destroys GH; it is an
age-gradient study with no sleep-restriction exposure in it at all.

## Verdict 2 — Final adult height: the exposure began too late to matter

**Expected height loss: 0.0 cm. Plausible interval 0.0 to 0.7 cm. Arithmetic ceiling 3.65 cm, which
would require complete arrest of all growth for three years.**

This is the bluntest verdict in the shard, and it rests on growth-plate timing rather than on any
sleep study.

**Only 3.65 cm of median growth existed to be lost.** From the WHO 2007 height-for-age reference for
boys (LMS table downloaded and parsed directly), median stature is 172.897 cm at age 16.0 y and
176.543 cm at 19.0 y. Residual median growth across the entire exposure window is **3.65 cm = 2.07%
of adult stature = 0.50 SD** (SD of 19-y stature = 7.298 cm). At exposure onset the subject had
already banked **97.9%** of his age-19 stature. Median annual velocity across the window collapses:
2.26 cm/y (16→17), 0.98 cm/y (17→18), **0.40 cm/y (18→19)** — by the second half of the exposure the
remaining growth is below the reproducibility of routine stadiometry.

**Growth plates close at bone age 17 in males** (Gupta 2020, the standard radiographic definition).
The empirical ceiling from that paper is more useful than the definition: among 3007 registry patients
with inflammatory bowel disease followed past the expected closure age — a population *enriched* for
delayed bone age, in which 80% were still growing — the **median total residual height gain after
chronological age 17 was 1.6 cm (Crohn's) and 1.3 cm (ulcerative colitis)**. A healthy, well-nourished
male has no more than that left, and probably less. Peak height velocity in boys occurs at 13.5 y and
the entire pubertal spurt contributes 30–31 cm (17–18% of final height) — essentially all of it
deposited before 16 (Abbassi 1998).

**The arithmetic.** Height cost = residual growth × fractional suppression of residual velocity.
3.65 cm × 5% = 0.18 cm; × 10% = 0.36 cm; × 20% = 0.73 cm; × 100% = 3.65 cm. Nothing in the literature
supports even a 10% velocity suppression from voluntary partial sleep restriction, and the mechanism
that would have to mediate it (24-h GH output) is not reduced — see Verdict 1.

**The strongest existing "sleep affects growth" evidence is a different exposure in different
children.** Pooled pre/post adenotonsillectomy for sleep-disordered breathing raises standardised
height by SMD 0.34 (95% CI 0.20–0.47; 10 studies, 363 children) and IGF-1 by SMD 0.53 (95% CI
0.33–0.73; 7 studies, 177 children) — Bonuck 2009. Three reasons it does not transport: the exposure
is obstructive breathing with intermittent hypoxaemia and increased work of breathing, not curtailed
time in bed; the children were prepubertal with most of their growth ahead of them; and the pooled
designs are uncontrolled pre/post, so regression to the mean and normal growth are not separated from
treatment effect. Decisively, the one **randomised** trial of that intervention (CHAT, n=464, ages
5–9.9 y) reports its anthropometric effect on **BMI z-score (+0.18, 95% CI 0.09–0.27, P<.0001)**, with
52% vs 21% of overweight children becoming obese; no stature contrast is extractable. Where sleep
treatment has been randomised, the gain is fat mass, not height.

**No cohort study of adolescent sleep duration and final adult height exists.** I searched 26 query
formulations. The two closest observational records are: a cross-sectional Beijing study of 5832
children aged 6–18 in which poor sleep habits associated with height-for-age z (β = −0.111, p<0.05
≈ 0.8 cm) — but in which sleep-disordered breathing associated *positively* with HAZ in 6–11 year olds
(β = +0.240), an internal contradiction that signals confounding (Chen 2025); and a 100-case /
200-control clinic study in which sufficient night sleep was protective against normal-variant short
stature (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.26–0.89), rated **critical** risk of bias because the exposure was recalled
by parents after the outcome was known and a co-reported OR of 0.02 for outdoor activity indicates
severe model instability. Both measure *attained* height in children still growing. Neither can
identify an effect of exposure at 16–19 on *final* height.

**Bluntly: this exposure began after ~98% of adult stature had already been laid down, and the final
two years of it contained 0.4 cm of median growth. Whatever else three years of 5–6 h sleep cost this
young man, adult height is not on the list.**

## Verdict 3 — Testosterone: a real 10–13% daytime deficit that reverses in three nights

**During exposure: −10.3% in mean daytime testosterone (95% CI −20.6% to −0.1%); −13.4% in the
14:00–22:00 window (95% CI −24.2% to −2.7%); Hedges' g = −0.52 (SE 0.23). n=10, mean age 24.3 y.**
**Persistent deficit after sleep normalises: 0% (95% CI not estimable — no study reports persistence;
two studies report complete reversal).**

The primary result is genuine. Eight nights of 5 h in bed (00:30–05:30, achieved TST 4h48m) versus 3
nights of 10 h lowered daytime (08:00–22:00) total testosterone from 18.4 (SD 3.8) to 16.5 (SD 2.8)
nmol/L, P=.049 (Leproult 2011). The age match is excellent — mean 24.3 (SD 4.3) y — and the exposure
duration is realistic. Note how marginal the significance is: the upper bound of the interval is
−0.1%, i.e. it barely excludes zero.

**Against the clinical reference range, this is not a clinically meaningful change.** Using the
authors' own conversion (÷0.0347), the rested mean is 530.3 ng/dL and the restricted mean 475.5 ng/dL.
The harmonized reference distribution for healthy nonobese men aged 19–39 (n=9054, CDC-calibrated) has
percentiles 2.5th = 264, 5th = 303, **50th = 531**, 95th = 852, 97.5th = 916 ng/dL (Travison 2017). So:

- the **rested** mean (530.3) is within 1 ng/dL of the population median (531);
- the **restricted** mean (475.5) sits at z = −0.40, roughly the **34th percentile**;
- it remains **1.80× the 264 ng/dL lower reference limit** used to diagnose hypogonadism;
- no participant was reported to have entered the hypogonadal range.

A 10–13% shift that moves a young man from the 50th to the 34th percentile of his own age
distribution, transiently, is a physiological state change and not a clinical finding. The authors
themselves benchmark it against ageing at "1% to 2% per year," i.e. the week of restriction is worth
about 5–10 years of ageing — but ageing is permanent and this is not (below).

**The replication record is genuinely mixed, and two of the three discrepant results point the same
way the subject's schedule does.**

- **Reynolds 2012** (n=14, men aged 27.4 ± 3.8): 5 nights at **4 h** in bed — a *harsher* dose — left
  total testosterone unchanged (F(1,168)=2.8, p=0.089), while SHBG fell (p<0.001), which would *raise*
  free testosterone.
- **Schmid 2012** (n=15, aged 27.1 ± 1.3): 2 nights at 4 h with a **late bedtime (02:45–07:00)** versus
  8 h (22:45–07:00) produced *no difference at all* in LH, testosterone or prolactin across a 15-h
  profile. But 4.5 h confined to the **first** half of the night (22:30–03:30) markedly lowered morning
  testosterone (P≤0.05, n=8). The authors' conclusion: "While sleep loss in the early part of the night
  does not affect T and PRL, early awakening and wakefulness during the second part of the night
  reduces morning circulating T and PRL concentrations." An independent review reaches the same
  reading: "sleep restriction only [lowers testosterone] if it occurs in the first half of the night"
  (Wittert 2014). **A college student loses sleep off the front of the night and wakes to an alarm.
  That is Schmid's null condition, not his positive one.**
- **NHANES 2011–2016** (n=8748): among men aged **20–40** — the closest age band available — sleep
  ≤6 h was associated with *higher* odds of **high** testosterone (OR 3.62, 95% CI 1.37–9.53)
  (Hernández-Pérez 2024). Almost certainly confounded and possibly reverse-causal, but it means the
  observational literature offers no support for a meaningful deficit in short-sleeping young men.
- **NHANES 2011–2012** (n=2295, median age 46 y): −5.85 ng/dL per hour of sleep lost (Patel 2019). At
  the subject's ~2.5 h weekday deficit that predicts **−14.6 ng/dL = 3.9%** of the 377 ng/dL cohort
  median. In the same model one unit of BMI is worth −6.18 ng/dL — a single BMI point outweighs an
  hour of sleep.

**Reversibility is the decisive finding, and it comes from the best exposure match in the shard.**
Killick 2015 studied 19 men (age 28.6 ± 2.0, range 19–49) with a mean **5.1 ± 0.9 years** of
lifestyle-driven weekday restriction — midweek sleep 6 h 12 min (range 5h18m–6h54m), weekend sleep
8 h 30 min, weekend extension 37.3% — which is the subject's pattern almost exactly. Three weekend
nights of 10 h in bed versus 6 h raised fasting morning testosterone by **+2.2 nmol/L (95% CI
+0.2 to +4.2; ≈ +63 ng/dL; P=0.03)**, in the analysed group (n=8) and in the younger subgroup alone
(n=5). Three nights undid a five-year exposure. Independently, after 24 h of continuous wakefulness
testosterone, cortisol and prolactin all fell and all "recovered basal levels after recovery sleep"
(P<0.001 for all, n=14 — Arnal 2016).

The mechanism explains why. Testosterone rises 65% across a night of sleep (15.3 → 25.3 nmol/L) and
53% across sleep displaced to the daytime (17.3 → 26.4 nmol/L), and falls during waking regardless of
clock time (n=7 — Axelsson 2005). The gonadal axis is a **sleep-driven accumulator**, not damaged
tissue. Short nights under-fill it; longer nights re-fill it. The subject's weekend and holiday sleep
of 7–8 h, with occasional 10–11 h nights, *is* the recovery condition Killick tested.

One caveat in the other direction: banking sleep does **not** work prospectively. Six nights of prior
sleep extension failed to protect testosterone or cortisol from a subsequent deprivation (Arnal 2016).
Catch-up repairs; it does not pre-pay.

## Verdict 4 — Cortisol / HPA axis: an evening-confined loss of rhythm amplitude, absent at 5 h

**Evening cortisol excess during exposure at 5–6 h/night: +5%, interval 0% to +25%. This interval is
my interpolation between doses, not a published CI — the two anchors are given below.**
**24-h total cortisol: +21% at 4 h in bed, 95% CI −4.7% to +46.7% — i.e. not significantly raised at
any measured dose.**
**Persistent change after sleep normalises: no evidence of any; morning cortisol is normal in men
chronically restricted for years.**

The best-quantified experiment is Guyon 2014 (n=13, randomised crossover, 2 nights of 4 h vs 2 nights
of 10 h in bed, 20-min sampling). At 4 h:

| Outcome | Effect | Interval (derived) | Reported p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evening total cortisol | **+30%** | 3.4% to 56.6% | P<.03 |
| Evening free (salivary) cortisol | **+200%** | 10.8% to 389% | P<.04 |
| Amplitude of the circadian cortisol decline | **−21%** | −42.0% to 0.0% | P<.05 |
| Overall ACTH | **+19%** | 2.2% to 35.8% | P<.03 |
| **Overall total cortisol** | **+21%** | **−4.7% to +46.7%** | **P=.10 (NS)** |

Intervals are derived from the reported p-value boundaries with df=12 (arithmetic in each YAML
record's `conversion_formula`); those from one-sided inequalities are conservative upper bounds on SE.
The +200% on free evening cortisol looks alarming but reflects a very low evening baseline denominator,
not a large absolute excursion. The pattern across the table is the important thing: **the daily total
is not significantly raised; the diurnal rhythm is flattened.** Guyon also report that "sleep
restriction was not associated with higher perceived stress" — this is a circadian-amplitude change,
not a stress response.

Consistent replication at 4 h: 5 nights of 4 h in bed raised mean cortisol 15.5% (337.4 ± 16.9 vs
292.2 ± 18.1 nmol/L, p=0.002) with the excursion concentrated in the evening — 18:00 cortisol +67%
(298.0 vs 178.1 nmol/L, F(1,97)=13.3, p<0.001), n=14 (Reynolds 2012). One night at 4 h versus a normal
8 h schedule raised 18:00–23:00 cortisol by 37% (p=0.03), and total deprivation by 45% (p=0.003), with
the evening quiescent period delayed at least 1 h (Leproult 1997).

**But at 5 h — the subject's actual dose — the signal disappears.** After 8 nights at 5 h in bed,
"daytime cortisol profiles were similar under both conditions," and the testosterone decline "was
associated with lower vigor scores but not with increased levels of cortisol" (n=10 — Leproult 2011).
And in men chronically restricted to ~6 h for a mean 5.1 years, "'catch-up' sleep increased morning
testosterone and **did not change morning cortisol**" (Killick 2015) — in chronic exposure there was
no residual HPA abnormality left to reverse.

**The canonical result overstates the real-world contrast.** Spiegel 1999 (6 nights of 4 h vs 6 nights
of **12 h**, n=11) is the most-cited sleep-cortisol paper: evening cortisol raised (p=0.0001), the
evening decline in salivary free cortisol "about six times slower," and the quiescent period delayed
"by nearly 1.5 hour." Its comparator is 12 h in bed, which no one habitually sleeps. When 9 of those
same 11 men were later studied at **8 h** in bed with identical procedures, "cortisol evening levels
observed under 8-hour bedtime condition were **intermediate** between those measured under 4-hour and
12-hour bedtime conditions" (Balbo 2010). Roughly half the headline effect is the 12-h arm being
supranormal, not the 4-h arm being pathological. Every lab study in this shard except Schmid 2012 and
Leproult 1997 uses a 10–12 h comparator, so this bias runs through the whole literature.

**The two anchors for my interval.** Upper: linear extrapolation from the cleanest 8-h-referenced
number (+37% at a 4 h deficit, Leproult 1997) gives 9.25%/h × 2–3 h ≈ **+19% to +28%** — but that is
one acute night and assumes no adaptation. Lower: the only direct chronic measurement at exactly the
subject's dose (5 h × 8 nights) is **0%** (Leproult 2011), and the only chronic real-world cohort is
also 0% (Killick 2015). The mass belongs near the bottom of that range, hence +5%.

**Chronic ceiling.** The only quantitative meta-analysis of chronic HPA activity in disturbed sleep
covers chronic insomnia *disorder*: SMD 0.50 (95% CI 0.21–0.80; 20 studies, 449 patients vs 357 good
sleepers, ages 18–70), rising to 0.67 (95% CI 0.15–1.18) in blood samples only (Dressle 2022). Two
things about it matter. First, that is the ceiling: years of clinically diagnosed insomnia buys SMD
~0.5. Second, and more telling, *within* that meta-analysis "a positive, but insignificant association
was found between the extent of objective sleep loss in insomnia patients and group differences in
cortisol levels" — the cortisol elevation in insomnia tracks hyperarousal and distress, not short
sleep duration. A student who chooses a late bedtime and sleeps well when given the chance is not in
that exposure category.

## Verdict 5 — Persistence after sleep normalises

| Axis | Recovery evidence | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Testosterone | 3 weekend nights of 10 h vs 6 h, in men with 5.1 y of the subject's exact pattern (n=8 analysed) | **+2.2 nmol/L (95% CI 0.2–4.2), P=0.03 — full reversal** (Killick 2015) |
| Testosterone / cortisol / prolactin | one recovery night after 24 h continuous wakefulness (n=14) | **"recovered basal levels," P<0.001 for all** (Arnal 2016) |
| Cortisol | morning cortisol in chronically restricted men (n=19) | **no change with catch-up — nothing to reverse** (Killick 2015) |
| Bone turnover | 6 nights at 5 h, then 3 weeks of recovery sampling (n=12) | **no change at any point, all p ≥ 0.63** (Swanson 2022) |
| Bone turnover | 9 nights at 5 h with vs without weekend recovery (n=20) | **no change, all p ≥ 0.3, no group × time interaction** (Depner 2021) |
| GH | no persistence study exists | moot — nothing is lost acutely (Verdict 1) |
| Prophylaxis | 6 nights of *prior* sleep extension, then deprivation (n=14) | **no protective effect on T or cortisol** (Arnal 2016) |

**No study in this domain reports a hormonal abnormality that outlives the exposure.** Balbo 2010
notes conflicting findings on whether recovery sleep normalises cortisol after *acute total*
deprivation, which is the one honest uncertainty here — but it concerns hours, not years.

## Verdict 6 — Pubertal timing and bone

**Pubertal timing: not a live outcome. The only human estimate is in girls (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.09–1.89
for early sexual maturation with insufficient sleep, mediated by obesity, n=959 girls); no significant
estimate was reported for the 459 boys in the same cohort, and the paired rat experiment found the
*opposite* direction — delayed preputial separation, 45 vs 42 days, p<0.05 (Gunawan 2024).** Pubertal
*onset* cannot be affected by an exposure beginning at 16, some 2.5 years after peak height velocity.

**Bone: 0% change in bone turnover markers at the subject's dose. Intervals not estimable — the
studies report nulls without point estimates.** Six nights at 5 h/night, with diet, physical activity,
posture and body weight controlled and *without* circadian disruption, produced "no statistically or
clinically significant change" in PINP (p=0.53), osteocalcin (p=0.66) or β-CTX (p=0.10), and nothing
through 3 weeks of recovery (n=12 — Swanson 2022). Nine nights at 5 h, with or without ad libitum
weekend recovery — the closest laboratory analogue of a weekday-restriction/weekend-catch-up schedule
— also produced nothing (n=20, all p ≥ 0.3 — Depner 2021).

The one positive result is instructive rather than alarming: P1NP fell 28.0% in men aged 20–27 (vs
18.2% in men 55–65, p<0.001 for the age difference) and sclerostin rose 22.9% — but the intervention
was 5.6 h sleep **plus a recurring 28-hour "day" in dim light for three weeks**, and bone resorption
did not change (CTX Δ = 0.03 ± 0.02 ng/mL, p=0.10), n=10 with only 6 in the young stratum (Swanson
2017). The authors name both components. When the isolating experiments removed the circadian
disruption, the effect vanished. The subject's exposure is short sleep on a stable 24-h schedule, not
forced desynchrony. **No densitometric BMD outcome exists for experimental sleep restriction in
adolescents — only turnover markers.**

## Honest accounting of the evidence base

Every experimental number above comes from a 24-h or multi-hour profile study in a small sample under
a laboratory protocol more extreme than the subject's real exposure.

| Study | n | Dose | Comparator | Age match |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Leproult 2011 (testosterone) | **10** | 5 h × 8 nights | 10 h | mean 24.3 y — good |
| Spiegel 2000 (GH) | **11** | 4 h × 6 nights | 12 h | young men |
| Spiegel 1999 (cortisol) | **11** | 4 h × 6 nights | 12 h | young men |
| Guyon 2014 (HPA) | **13** | 4 h × 2 nights | 10 h | young men |
| Reynolds 2012 | **14** | 4 h × 5 nights | 10 h | mean 27.4 y |
| Schmid 2012 | **15** / 8 | 4 h × 2 nights | **8 h** | mean 27.1 y |
| Killick 2015 | **19** / 8 analysed | habitual 6h12m, 5.1 y | 10 h catch-up | mean 28.6 y, range 19–49 |
| Arnal 2016 | **14** | total deprivation | habitual | young men |
| Axelsson 2005 | **7** | displaced sleep | — | 22–32 y |
| Brandenberger 2004 | **10 + 11** | night work | day work | adults |
| Swanson 2017 (bone) | **10** (6 young) | 5.6 h + 28-h day × 3 wk | baseline | 20–27 and 55–65 y |
| Swanson 2022 (bone) | **12** | 5 h × 6 nights | 8 h | young men |
| Depner 2021 (bone) | **20** (3 controls) | 5 h × 9 nights | 9 h | mixed sex |

**Transient physiological adaptation vs durable endocrine damage — one line each:**

- **GH redistribution:** transient adaptation, and explicitly framed as such by the primary authors
  ("adaptation mechanisms are operative during chronic partial sleep loss").
- **Testosterone −10 to −13%:** transient adaptation. Reverses in 3 nights after 5 years of exposure.
- **Evening cortisol / flattened diurnal slope:** transient adaptation, dose-dependent, undetectable at
  5 h in the one protocol run at that dose, and absent in chronically restricted men.
- **Bone turnover:** no effect at all once circadian disruption is removed.
- **Final adult height:** not an endocrine question. It is a growth-plate timing question, and the
  answer is that the plates were closing as the exposure began.

**Three structural biases, all inflating the apparent harm:** (1) comparators of 10–12 h in bed rather
than 8 h, worth roughly half the headline cortisol effect (Balbo 2010); (2) doses of 4 h rather than
5–6 h, at which the cortisol signal is present rather than absent; (3) acute 1–8 night protocols that
cannot capture the adaptation that Killick 2015 documents in men exposed for years. Set against these,
one bias inflates *nothing*: none of these studies included a 16–19 year old, so the transportability
of every effect to the subject's higher baseline testosterone and peak slow-wave sleep is assumed, not
measured.
