# What three years of short sleep actually did to you

## 1. Bottom line

Not catastrophic. Against an age-appropriate requirement you accumulated about **2,921 hours** of sleep debt (95% interval 961-4,831) from age 16, averaging **2.8 h short per night**. But only about **843 hours** of that is attributable to what changed at 16: your self-described *good* schedule of 6-8 h at ages 14-16 was already roughly 2 h below an age-appropriate requirement, so most of the debt would have accrued anyway.

Measured full-scale IQ today versus after eight weeks of adequate sleep: **-0.7 [-3.4, +0.3] points**. Permanent change in adult cognitive ability: **0.0 points, 95% interval -1.7 to 0.0** — the most likely value is exactly no loss, with a 5% probability of losing more than 1 point and 0.4% of losing more than 3. Life expectancy: **-0.06 months** (-0.27 to -0.02), i.e. days, not years. On a like-for-like basis this exposure ranks **3 of 8** against smoking, obesity, inactivity, alcohol and poor diet — mid-band, with all of them inside 1-3 months.

The real damage is not your brain or your lifespan. It is **-0.09 GPA points** (-0.12 to -0.04) of coursework you did not learn, which does not come back, plus a current vigilance deficit of **Hedges' g -0.79** that does. Prescription: **8.9 h in bed** (not 8 h of sleep — 8.9 h in bed to *get* ~8 h), seven days a week, plus a short afternoon nap if you want to close the last half hour. Expect 69% recovery by week 4 and 89% by week 52. There is a 21% chance you have a treatable sleep disorder rather than only a schedule problem, which is worth ruling out.

## 3. The prescription

**Maintenance dose: 8.9 hours in bed, every night, seven days a week.** That is time in bed, not sleep. Sleep efficiency in healthy young adults is about 87.5%, so a sleep target needs roughly 45-60 minutes more time in bed than the target itself. This is the number that stops new debt accruing.

There is a hard ceiling you should know about. Habitual **nocturnal** sleep saturates near 7.9 h; the frequently quoted 8.9 h figure was obtained in a protocol that included a daytime nap opportunity. There is a 64% probability your own requirement is above what night-time sleep alone can deliver, in which case the remainder has to come from a scheduled nap. That is why the prescription is a time-in-bed target *plus* an optional nap rather than a single number.

**Weekend catch-up alone cannot work, and this is arithmetic rather than physiology.** Repaying a weekday deficit of 2.8 h over two weekend nights would require 15.2 h of sleep per weekend night against a single-night ceiling of about 10.2 h. At best a weekend repays 29% of the week's deficit. Sleeping in on Saturday is worth doing and is not a solution.

**Do not read the 2,921-hour debt as a repayment schedule.** Your maximum sustainable nightly surplus over requirement is a fraction of an hour, so clearing the ledger arithmetically would take 11+ years. That is not what happens. Function recovers on a time constant of 16 days (5-47) largely independently of how many ledger hours accumulated. The hours figure measures the size of the historical exposure, not the length of the repayment.

Two things that do work, and one that does not:

- **Banking works forwards.** Sleep extension *before* a known restriction period measurably buffers the subsequent impairment. Extending sleep the week before finals is supported; catching up after is much less efficient.
- **Naps are load-bearing, not optional garnish.** They are the only route past the nocturnal ceiling.
- **Sleeping much more than your requirement does not help and is not the U-shaped risk people claim.** The long-sleep limb of the mortality and diabetes curves largely disappears under objective measurement and under genetic instrumentation, so your occasional 10-11 h weekend nights are recovery, not a risk factor.

The honest split: **one thing on this list is permanent, and it is not a biological injury.** It is the material you did not learn while you were too tired to encode it. Everything with a biological mechanism either has reverted or reverts on a timescale of days to weeks once you sleep adequately.

Two important caveats in the other direction. First, *reversible* is established over the days-to-weeks windows that studies have actually observed; the longest chronic-restriction experiment ever run is 42 nights against your roughly 1,040, and 39% of the recovery literature followed people for three nights or fewer. Every time someone extended the observation window they found *more* incomplete recovery, never less. Second, "no evidence of permanent change" is not the same as "evidence of no permanent change": nobody has ever imaged or tested habitual 5-6 h sleepers aged 16-19 more than once, so the reassuring nulls are extrapolations downward past the range anyone sampled.

## 5. Reasoning, outcome by outcome

### Dose: how much sleep you actually lost

Your reported hours were not used as measured hours. Self-reported habitual sleep exceeds objectively measured sleep by roughly an hour on average, reports cluster on whole and half hours, and it is unresolved whether you meant time in bed or time asleep. The model corrects the habitual level while preserving your within-person contrasts (so a reported 3.5 h exam night is not 'corrected' upward to 6 h), applies a school/break/exam calendar rather than a flat average, and credits the ~14-16 weeks a year you slept on an ad-lib schedule. Estimated actual sleep over the exposure window: **5.59 [4.78, 6.68] h**, against an inferred requirement of 8.18 [6.61, 9.75] h.

59% of nights fell below 6 h and 36% below 5 h.

The single most important framing point in this whole report: **the referent matters more than the exposure.** Comparing you to an age-appropriate requirement gives a large debt. Comparing you to *your own prior schedule* gives roughly a quarter of it, because 6-8 h at ages 14-16 was also below what a 15-year-old needs. If you have been carrying a sense that junior year broke something, the arithmetic says junior year made an existing deficit moderately worse rather than creating one.

### Cognition now

Vigilance is the most affected function and the best evidenced. Pooling 5 within-subject experimental restriction studies (including one four-dose ladder in 15-19 year olds, the closest age match that exists) gives a pooled effect of g -1.12 [-1.94, -0.34] at the studied doses, with substantial heterogeneity (tau 0.69). Transported to your dose, the estimate for you is **g -0.79 [-3.62, +0.55]**.

Be careful how you read that interval: it spans 'no deficit' to 'severe'. That width is not sloppiness, it is the single most important fact in this section. Vulnerability to sleep loss is strongly trait-like (intraclass correlation ~0.675, stable over years), so the population average is a poor guide to any individual. The interval quoted is the *predictive* interval for one person, not the confidence interval on the average, which is much narrower and would be the wrong number to give you.

Higher-order cognition is a different story, and the contrast is the reason the IQ answer is small. In the same literature, effect sizes fall steeply as tasks move from vigilance toward reasoning: pooled lapses around −0.5 to −0.8, executive function around −0.32, long-term memory around −0.19, and reasoning around −0.13. Two randomised experiments in exactly your age band returned nulls on higher-order measures: working memory was unaffected at 7 h versus 10 h in bed, and a 2024 crossover trial found cognitive effects only in adolescents with overweight or obesity, reporting that 'no differences emerged for adolescents with healthy weight'.

### Intelligence

The decisive constraint is direct measurement. binks1999 WAIS-R short form after 34-36 h total sleep deprivation found +3.6 points (SE 2.77) — no decrement at all — after a harsher exposure than yours, and its interval excludes a decrement worse than about 1.8 points.

The common error here is multiplying a vigilance effect size by 15 and calling the result IQ points. That is wrong by roughly a factor of five: a reaction-time task shares only about 20-25% of its variance with general intelligence, and the measured reasoning-to-lapses ratio inside the largest meta-analysis is 0.164. Applying the naive 1:1 mapping to your exposure would yield a double-digit IQ loss, which is falsified by the direct measurement above.

Splitting crystallized from fluid: crystallized ability and full-scale IQ show no detectable decrement; the state deficit sits in fluid and processing-speed measures. Permanent change is **0.0 points with a 95% interval of -1.65 to 0.00**. The probability of a permanent loss exceeding 3 points is 0.4%.

That number is built from two separate possibilities rather than one, because they are different claims. The first is that part of your current state deficit simply fails to reverse (contributing +0.00 [-0.44, +0.00] points). The second is a developmental effect — that restriction during ongoing brain maturation left a mark unrelated in size to how tired you are today, modelled with a 8% probability of occurring and contributing +0.00 [-1.58, +0.00] points. The developmental pathway is modelled as an explicit possibility rather than assumed absent, because no study has imaged or tested habitual 5-6 h sleepers aged 16-19 more than once. Its prior is an assumption, not a measurement, and it is what makes the permanent interval asymmetric toward harm.

### Academic achievement — the part that actually cost you something

Quasi-experimental evidence (school start-time changes, time-zone and sunset-time instruments, daylight-saving discontinuities) gives about 0.15 SD of achievement per hour of habitual sleep. Two figures are given because extrapolation matters: linear in dose gives -0.39 [-0.94, -0.09] SD, and a saturating form gives -0.18 [-0.34, -0.05] SD. In GPA terms, -0.09 [-0.12, -0.04] points.

Identified over sleep changes of 20-40 min; applying it to a 3 h deficit is a large linear extrapolation. The saturating figure is the one to believe.

This is the largest real cost of the exposure and the only one that does not reverse. Sleeping properly from now on restores your capacity to learn; it does not retroactively teach you the material you encoded badly at 16 and 17.

### Mood and psychiatric risk

The observational literature looks alarming and mostly is not applicable to you. The key structure is an exposure gradient of roughly tenfold: insomnia disorder carries an odds ratio near 2.8, behaviourally imposed short sleep near 1.2, and genetically instrumented sleep duration is null. Your exposure is the weakest of the three. Between 55% and 70% of the observational association is non-causal — reverse causation plus confounding plus criterion contamination, where depression scales count fatigue items that short sleep moves directly. In the best instrumental-variable study the mood items alone were null (d -0.03) while the fatigue items carried the effect.

Causal symptom effect at your dose: -0.043 [-0.146, -0.007] d. Absolute excess risk of a major depressive episode: +1.00 [+0.04, +4.18] percentage points.

### Metabolic, cardiovascular, immune, endocrine

All four are real while the exposure is running and revert once it stops. Type 2 diabetes: applying the dose-response per-hour risk ratio and then the fraction that survives genetic instrumentation gives a relative risk of 1.038 [1.008, 1.091] while exposed, and an absolute lifetime change of +0.08 [+0.00, +0.30] percentage points against a baseline lifetime risk of 40%. Cardiovascular: Roughly 33% of the crude association survives adjustment (20-50%), total CVD is null in the largest meta-analysis (RR 1.03, 0.93-1.15), and the only age-exact evidence points the opposite way. Absolute change at 19 is indistinguishable from zero.

Immune effects are real and reversible, but reversibility is dose-dependent in a way worth knowing: the one study that varied recovery sleep while holding restriction constant found abnormalities persisting after 8 h of recovery and resolving only after 10 h, or after a nap plus 8 h. Your occasional 10-11 h weekend nights were doing more work than you thought.

On the things people worry about most: 24-hour growth hormone output is unchanged because the blunted sleep-onset pulse is compensated by waking pulses, and slow-wave sleep — when most sleep-onset growth hormone is released — is preserved, indeed slightly increased, under restriction, while stage 2 and REM absorb the loss. Final adult height is unaffected: a male has only about 3.65 cm of median growth left between 16 and 19, and 0.40 cm between 18 and 19. Testosterone fell about 10% during the exposure and rebounds within days.

### Lifespan, and why the number is so small

Life expectancy change: **-0.06 [-0.27, -0.02] months**. The reason this is days rather than years is a single arithmetic fact that dominates every long-run channel: the probability a US male dies between exact ages 16 and 19 is about 0.0025. A hazard ratio applied only across that window therefore costs almost nothing no matter how large it is — tripling the hazard for three years costs about 107 days. The same ratio applied for the rest of life costs 50-60 times more. Which of those applies depends entirely on whether a closed adolescent exposure permanently shifts the hazard, and **no study of any design has ever measured that**. The model assumes it mostly does not, on the grounds that every mechanism anyone has measured reverts.

INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE. No study of any design measures dementia risk from an adolescent exposure; the youngest exposure measurement in the literature is age 50.6. Eight Mendelian randomisation analyses give zero support for sleep duration causing Alzheimer's disease, and the short-sleep-to-Alzheimer's meta-analytic estimate is exactly null (RR 1.02, 0.76-1.36). No number is defensible; the channel is excluded from the damage total rather than assigned a value.

One channel does act inside the window rather than in old age, and it was missing from the first version of this analysis: drowsy driving. Conditional on being a typical licensed US male teenage driver it costs about 0.06 months (0.01-0.14), and zero if you do not drive. Small in absolute terms, but comparable to the entire chronic-disease contribution, and it is the one mortality risk that was live at the time rather than fifty years away.

## 6. What would change this answer

### The surviving objections from adversarial review

Three independent critics were given the full repository and instructed to break it: a methodologist, a domain skeptic, and a contrarian mandated to argue that the damage is negligible. They filed 49 numbered objections. Two full rework loops followed. Every objection is resolved in writing in `reports/objection_resolution.md`; the material corrections were:

- The sleep-need referent was being taken from a study measuring maximal sleep *capacity* with a nap opportunity, and a stray clip in the code made the parameter unable to move below 8 h while the value *reported* as the requirement bypassed the clip. Fixing this reduced the debt by ~400 h and is the largest single correction in the project.
- The mortality channel used a categorical short-versus-normal risk ratio when dose-specific values were available, which inflated the life-expectancy loss roughly fourfold.
- The IQ estimate averaged four routes that were neither four nor independent, one of which produced an upper interval limit implying you would test 7 points *above* your own rested self. Restructured; the interval narrowed from ±7 to about −3.4 to +0.3.
- The comparator ranking discounted this exposure for cessation while not discounting the comparators, which is how it originally came out 'least harmful of eight'. On a symmetric basis it is 3rd of 8.
- Two harm channels were missing entirely: academic achievement, and drowsy-driving mortality. Both are now included.

What survives and is not fixed: the publication-bias corrections are computed and reported but not propagated into the posterior, because with 1-5 studies per parameter the estimators are too unstable to correct with. Both that and the exclusion of qualitatively-reported null findings from inverse-variance pools bias this report **toward overstating harm**, and neither is corrected.

### Model dependence

The multiverse enumerates 18 branches across three axes: whether your reports meant time in bed or time asleep, which of three defensible self-report calibrations applies, and which of three sleep-need referents is right. **Every branch agrees you accumulated a real deficit** — the sign is completely robust — but the magnitude spans 1,570 to 3,736 hours, a factor of 2.4. Nightly deficit spans 1.51 to 3.59 h.

So: treat the direction and the ordering of these findings as solid, and treat every specific number as good to one significant figure.

### What to measure, in priority order

The variance decomposition is unusually clear about this. **67% of the uncertainty in the cognitive estimate is between-person heterogeneity in how people respond to sleep loss**, not uncertainty about the literature. Between-person heterogeneity in the experimental response dominates. Trait-like vulnerability to sleep loss has an ICC of about 0.675 and is stable over years, so the population effect size is a poor guide to any one person. This is the entire justification for measuring his own response rather than reading a number off the literature.

And the prescription is dominated by a different unknown: The maintenance dose is a deterministic function of individual sleep need, which is currently a population prior with an SD of 0.70 h resting on 15 subjects. An ad-lib sleep protocol would collapse this interval almost entirely.

Two measurements, in this order: an ad-lib sleep protocol to fix his own sleep need, and a repeated within-person vigilance test to fix his own sensitivity. Together they remove most of the width in every number in this report.

## 7. The four-week measurement protocol

This converts every population number above into a personal one. It is worth more than anything else in this report.

**Weeks 1-2, measure your baseline.** Sleep diary every morning plus a wearable or actigraph every night. Fourteen nights is the minimum: a single night's estimate of habitual sleep has an intraclass correlation of only 0.24, and you need 13 nights for a reliability of 0.80 and 15 for a standard error of measurement under 20 minutes. Record lights-out, estimated sleep onset latency, wake time, and out-of-bed time, because time in bed minus time asleep is the quantity the prescription hinges on. Do **not** trust a wearable's sleep-onset-latency estimate; current devices underestimate it, which is falsely reassuring on the single most diagnostic variable. Take latency from your diary.

Complete three instruments once: the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, the Insomnia Severity Index, and the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire. Note the asymmetry in how to read them: a *normal* Epworth does not argue against a circadian disorder (about 85% of confirmed cases score in the normal range), but an Insomnia Severity Index below 7 is the strongest rule-out available.

**Weeks 3-4, measure your sleep need.** An ad-lib protocol: 12 hours of sleep opportunity per night, no alarm, dark room, for at least 9 consecutive nights. Sleep duration will be long for the first 3-4 nights and then asymptote; the asymptote is your requirement. Three nights is not enough — the published protocol took 4 days just to discharge one hour of debt and 9 nights to reach a stable value. A school break is the only realistic window for this.

**Throughout, measure your own sensitivity.** A validated psychomotor vigilance test, five times a day at fixed clock times. Use **mean reciprocal reaction time** as the primary outcome, not lapse count: it is the most sensitive metric (d_z 1.21 versus 0.91 for lapses) and lapse counts have poor test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation 0.51). Discard the first five sessions as practice; speed shows no practice effect but accuracy does.

**Honest power calculation, because you should know what this can and cannot detect.** With a within-person rested standard deviation of about 6% of your own mean reciprocal reaction time, and an expected effect of about 0.21 s⁻¹ for a 2 h/night contrast, you need **about 31 analysed nights per condition** — 62 total — for 80% power at alpha 0.05 using a paired t-test, and 20-28 calendar weeks once night-to-night dependence is modelled. A four-week design has only 11-16% power for a 2 h contrast. **So a four-week protocol will reliably measure your sleep need and your habitual duration, and will not reliably measure your vigilance sensitivity.** If you want the second, plan on six blocks of 14 nights with a 3 h contrast.

**When to see a sleep physician rather than just going to bed earlier.** There is a 21% probability (12-32%) that something treatable is present, and the composition matters: insomnia ~18%, delayed sleep-wake phase disorder ~2%, obstructive sleep apnea ~1%. Go if, during the two diary weeks, you routinely take more than 60 minutes to fall asleep on nights when you have the opportunity, or if you cannot fall asleep at your target bedtime even with a full opportunity and no alarm the next day. That specific pattern points to a circadian disorder, for which the treatment is completely different from 'go to bed earlier' — timed morning bright light plus low-dose melatonin taken hours before bedtime, which in adolescents took the proportion still meeting diagnostic criteria from 82% to 13%.

One thing the weekday/weekend gap does **not** tell you: it has essentially no diagnostic value for a circadian disorder (likelihood ratio 1.13), and a 2 h weekend delay is the worldwide adolescent norm. Your own social jetlag of about 1.75 h sits at roughly the 45th percentile for your age. You are unremarkable on circadian misalignment and unusual only on duration.

## 8. How this was produced, and the evidence base

- **597 evidence records** across 21 domain shards, carrying **1999 effect estimates**, of which 1016 have a usable standard error. Tier mix: {'T1': 212, 'T2': 26, 'T3': 27, 'T4': 31, 'T5': 95, 'TX': 206}.
- **Citation verification: 593/597 identifiers resolved against Crossref and PubMed with the resolved title matched against the recorded citation**; the remaining 4 are official statistical products with no DOI, verified by domain. 0 unverified. No fabricated citation was detected.
- **Blinded re-extraction** of 140 targets (38.7% of records with poolable effects, including all 33 designated influential studies) by six independent agents that could not see the first team's work. Agreement: ICC 0.7216 raw, 0.9706 sign-reconciled; kappa 0.8958 on evidence tier, 0.3601 on risk of bias.
- **16 of 16 acceptance gates pass**, with failures described rather than relaxed, in `reports/gate_log.md`. Risk of bias carries no numeric weight anywhere in the model because its inter-rater agreement (kappa 0.3601) is too low to justify weighting on it.
- **43 tests pass**, including regression tests that reproduce published pooled estimates, agreement between two independent posterior computations (exact grid marginalisation and NUTS, agreeing to 0.0005 on the pooled mean), and reproduction of the published US male life expectancy at 19 to 0.0002 years.
- **1,000,000 Monte Carlo draws**, fixed seed 20260728, everything regenerable with `make all`.

Bias diagnostics: E-values are 1.49 for the mortality association and 1.40 for the per-hour diabetes association. An unmeasured confounder associated with both short sleep and the outcome by a risk ratio of roughly 1.5 would explain away the mortality association entirely. Depression, socioeconomic position, shift work, physical inactivity and undiagnosed illness are all plausible at that strength, which is why the residual causal fraction is modelled at 0.25 rather than 1.0.
