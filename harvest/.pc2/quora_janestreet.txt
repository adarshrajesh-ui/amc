A combination of basic probability, counting, and elementary stochastic processes questions. I first interviewed with Jane Street soon after I turned 17- I knew more than enough math at that time to do well. Nothing tricky.
Making markets on Fermi-esque questions, and your ability to understand information asymmetry and adverse selection are also indirectly tested.
I definitely don't agree with the comment regarding the difficulty of the interview process. These days, I work at a UHFT firm, and we frequently interview JS/SIG employees as well as last quant interns, and their performance is subpar at best; none have passed the first round. Largely, they fail to generalize their sometimes correct intuition into tackling optimal control, decision theory, or differential games.
For example, the question that Michael stated above is a special class of discrete stochastic control, finite horizon, problems which one can trivially formulate the HJB equations for deriving an optimal policy at each point in time.
A much more interesting question, relevant in the UHFT space, is an approximation algorithm that produces a greedy policy that bounds the optimal policy in sublinear time. Or, more relevant, same question but the die is biased- you only have a prior distribution p.

Agree with[Eugene Chen](https://www.quora.com/profile/Eugene-Chen-2)- the interviews were generally probability/expected value questions with a nod towards game theory or optimal strategies. They got harder in each successive round. If you want a concrete example, here was one of my first round interview questions when I interviewed in 2012 (paraphrased):
> Suppose we play a game in which I give you a die to roll and offer to pay you in dollars the number that you roll (if you roll a 3, I'll pay you $3). I also stipulate that if you're dissatisfied with the number that you roll on your first try, I'll let you roll the die again with the same offer. I will allow a maximum of 3 "do-overs", though you can elect to stop after any roll.
> > How much would you pay me to play this game?
I should also add that in general, it seemed to me that you needed to either solve or make significant progress towards the solution for each interview problem in order to advance to the next round. I got 'dinged' in my 4th round interview despite solving 3/4 problems correctly. Talk about high standards!

First off, I can definitely relate to how you are feeling right now. Though I did participate in math competitions in high school, I didn't do phenomenally well in them, at least compared to the majority of the Jane Street intern class last summer.However, Jane Street does not select its interns because they did well in math competitions alone. Jane Street wants interns with a variety of strengths and weaknesses. However, there are a few unifying threads; all successful interns (and full timers) I met at Jane Street enjoyed thinking about challenging problems, had tons of intellectual curiosity, and worked extremely hard. Notice that these traits happen to be found more frequently in math olympiad winners than average students.
Jane Street's interview process attempts to find students that have the traits I listed above. However, like all interview processes, it isn't perfect. Students that have a background studying combinatorics, expected value, and mathematical puzzles have a significant advantage. In an ideal world, Jane Street interviewers would give each applicant problems that would challenge them but still be doable with their current knowledge. Interviewers want to see how applicantsthink throughchallenging problems; most of my interviews were conversations where I explained my thought process to my interviewer, and she gave me hints if I got stuck. However, for me to get through these interviews, I used background knowledge I obtained from reading orworking the problemsin these sources:
* Introduction to Counting and ProbabilityandIntermediate Counting and Probabilityby David Patrick from the Art of Problem Solving ([Art of Problem Solving](http://www.artofproblemsolving.com/store)). These books cover most of what you need to know for the probability/expected value questions in the interviews; if you don't have significant olympiad experience on your resume, I doubt the questions you are asked are harder than those in the intermediate book.
* Cracking the Coding Interviewby Gayle Laakmann McDowell. Though this book is mainly used for computer science interviews, some of the questions I got asked in my Jane Street interviews had an algorithmic flavor to them. This book also has a nice collection of puzzles, detailed solutions if you get stuck, and useful tips for solving puzzles in general.
* Thinking Fast and Slowby Daniel Kahneman. This book is an excellent introduction to all of the biases we naturally have. If you can recognize when you are likely to be biased, you can make more rational decisions, which is exactly what Jane Street interviewers want you to do :) For reference, all 2015 winterns got sent a copy of this book.
* AIME problems and solutions on[Art of Problem Solving](http://www.artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php/AIME_Problems_and_Solutions). There's definitely lots of overlap between this and the first AoPS source. If you want more practice on appropriate combinatorics problems, this is the place to go. I would recommend not going past #10 on the tests if you want to stay in the range of Jane Street interview difficulty for your background.
I listed the sources in order of importance. If you stuck to a reasonable schedule, you could definitely get through the first two in a year. If you really try to work through most of the problems in the counting and probability AoPS books (and only look at the solutions if you get stuck!), you will have a great chance at acing the JS interviews. That being said, keep in mind that Jane Streetvalues diversityand doesn't just select for raw intelligence. They want people like you with computer science skills that can be hybrid trader-devs, they want people with leadership skills that can think about the bigger picture, and they (of course) want people with "uber genius" skills that can create the next cutting-edge model to trade on. And even if you prepare and don't get the offer, these sources are generally great to make you abetter applicant for anything.Knowing the basics of probability, problem solving, and psychology will likely be helpful wherever you end up.
